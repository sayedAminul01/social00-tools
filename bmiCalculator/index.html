What Is BMI, Really?

Body Mass Index is a simple ratio of your weight to the square of your height (kg/m²). It was designed in the 1830s as a way to study body-weight patterns across large populations, not to assess any one individual's health. It's popular because it's cheap and easy to calculate from just two numbers — not because it's a precise health measurement.

The WHO BMI Categories

The World Health Organization defines four adult ranges: Underweight (below 18.5), Normal weight (18.5–24.9), Overweight (25–29.9), and Obese (30 and above). These thresholds are population averages used for public-health screening, not personalized cutoffs for any one body.

What BMI Doesn't Measure

BMI can't distinguish muscle from fat, doesn't account for bone density or age, ignores sex differences in body composition, and says nothing about where fat is distributed on your body — a factor that matters more for health risk than total weight alone. Two people with the same BMI can have very different body compositions and very different health profiles.

Why BMI Misleads Athletes and Muscular People

Muscle is denser than fat, so a muscular person can weigh more per inch of height without carrying excess body fat. BMI can't tell the difference, which is why athletes, bodybuilders, and other muscular individuals are routinely labeled "overweight" or "obese" by BMI alone despite having low body fat.

BMI for Children, Teens & Pregnancy

Adult BMI cutoffs don't apply to children or teens, who are still growing — their BMI needs to be plotted on age- and sex-specific percentile charts, not measured against the adult ranges above. BMI also isn't a meaningful metric during pregnancy, when weight gain is expected and healthy. Both cases call for guidance from a doctor or pediatrician, not this calculator.

A Screening Tool, Not a Diagnosis

Doctors use BMI as one quick, cheap starting point among many — alongside waist circumference, blood pressure, lab work, family history, and a physical exam — to decide whether a closer look is worthwhile. It flags a number worth discussing; it does not diagnose a condition. If your result concerns you, the right next step is a conversation with a healthcare provider, not a verdict from this page.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is BMI?

Body Mass Index is a simple ratio of your weight to the square of your height (kg/m²). It's a quick, population-level screening number, not a direct measurement of body fat.

Is BMI accurate?

Not for individuals. BMI is a rough, population-level screening heuristic, not an individual diagnostic measure — it can't tell muscle from fat, so it regularly misclassifies muscular people as "overweight" and can miss high body fat in people with low muscle mass.

What are the WHO BMI categories?

For adults: Underweight is below 18.5, Normal weight is 18.5–24.9, Overweight is 25–29.9, and Obese is 30 or above. These ranges apply to adults only.

Why does BMI mislead athletes and muscular people?

Muscle is denser than fat, so a muscular person can weigh more per inch of height without carrying excess body fat. BMI can't tell the difference and often labels athletes and bodybuilders as "overweight" or "obese" even when their body fat is low.

Can I use this calculator for a child's or teen's BMI?

No. Children and teens are still growing, so a single adult BMI cutoff doesn't apply to them. Their BMI needs to be plotted on age- and sex-specific growth percentile charts, which is something a pediatrician handles.

Is my height and weight data sent anywhere?

No. Every calculation runs locally in your browser using JavaScript — nothing you type is sent to a server, logged, or stored anywhere, which matters since height and weight are personal health information.
