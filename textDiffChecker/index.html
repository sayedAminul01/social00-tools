What Is a Text Diff?

A text diff compares two versions of a text and reports exactly what's different between them, line by line. Instead of just telling you "these two texts are different," a good diff tells you which lines are identical in both, which lines were removed from the original, and which new lines were added — so you can see the actual edit, not just that one happened.

How LCS-Based Diffing Works

This tool finds the Longest Common Subsequence (LCS) of lines shared between the original and changed text — the longest run of lines that appears in both, in the same order, though not necessarily consecutively. Every line that's part of that shared sequence is "unchanged." Any line from the original that falls outside it is marked removed, and any line from the changed text that falls outside it is marked added.

Why LCS Beats Naive Comparison

A naive diff just compares line 1 to line 1, line 2 to line 2, and so on. Insert a single line near the top and every line after it appears to "change," even though nothing else actually moved. LCS-based diffing re-aligns the two texts around the lines they genuinely share, so inserting or deleting one line shows up as exactly one addition or removal — the rest of the text stays correctly marked as unchanged.

Case & Whitespace Insensitive Comparison

Sometimes two lines should count as "the same" even if they aren't byte-for-byte identical. Turn on case-insensitive comparison to treat "Hello" and "hello" as matching, or ignore leading/trailing whitespace to treat a re-indented or re-formatted line as unchanged. Both options change how the LCS algorithm decides whether two lines match — not just how the result looks.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a line-based text diff?

A line-based diff compares two blocks of text line by line and reports which lines are unchanged, which were removed from the original, and which were added in the new version — instead of just saying the two texts are different.

How does the LCS diff algorithm work?

It finds the Longest Common Subsequence — the longest run of lines that appear in both texts in the same relative order. Any line in the original that isn't part of that shared sequence is marked removed, and any line in the changed text that isn't part of it is marked added.

Why is this better than a naive line-by-line comparison?

A naive comparison just checks line 1 against line 1, line 2 against line 2, and so on, so inserting or deleting a single line makes every line after it look changed. LCS-based diffing re-aligns the two texts around their shared lines, so a single insertion or deletion shows up as exactly one added or removed line.

What do the case-insensitive and whitespace toggles do?

They change what counts as "the same line" during comparison. With case-insensitive on, "Hello" and "hello" are treated as matching. With whitespace-trimming on, leading and trailing spaces or tabs on a line are ignored, so reformatted lines don't get flagged as changed.

What can I use a text diff checker for?

Common uses include comparing two drafts of a document, reviewing changes between versions of code, checking contract redlines against an original, and spotting exactly what a collaborator edited in shared notes.

Is my text uploaded anywhere?

No. Both texts are compared entirely locally in your browser using JavaScript — nothing you type is ever sent to a server or stored.
