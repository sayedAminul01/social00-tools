What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is what happens when the interest a balance earns is added back to that balance, so future interest is calculated on a bigger number every period. Leave $1,000 at 5% annual interest for a year and you earn $50. Leave it another year and you earn 5% of $1,050 — $52.50, not $50 — because last year's interest is now earning interest of its own. Over long stretches of time, this "interest on interest" effect is what makes compound growth accelerate rather than stay flat.

Compound vs. Simple Interest: A Worked Example

Simple interest only ever applies to the original principal: Interest = P × r × t. Put $10,000 at 5% for 10 years under simple interest and you get 10,000 × 0.05 × 10 = $5,000 in interest, for a total of $15,000. Under annual compound interest, the same $10,000 at 5% for 10 years grows to $16,288.95 — $1,288.95 more — purely because each year's interest was added to the base before the next year's interest was calculated.

Why Compounding Frequency Matters

At the same nominal (stated) annual rate, compounding more often gives a slightly higher effective annual rate, because each compounding period locks in a bit of interest sooner, which then itself starts earning interest. A 6% nominal rate compounded annually has an effective annual rate of exactly 6%. Compounded monthly, it works out to about 6.17%; compounded daily, about 6.18%. The gains from compounding more frequently get smaller and smaller the more often you already compound — the jump from annual to monthly matters far more than the jump from monthly to daily.

The Power of Regular Contributions

Adding a fixed amount on a schedule — the same idea behind dollar-cost averaging — lets every contribution start compounding from the moment it's made, not just the initial lump sum. Contribute $200 a month for 20 years at a steady 7% annual rate, compounded monthly, and you'd put in $48,000 of your own money but end up with a projected $104,185.33 — more than half the final balance came from growth, not from what you deposited. The earlier and more consistently contributions start, the more time each one has to compound.

How This Calculator Works

Rather than relying on a single closed-form formula (which gets messy once contribution frequency and compounding frequency don't match), this tool simulates growth one compounding period at a time: it applies interest to the current balance, then adds any contribution due that period, and repeats for every period across your full time horizon. That's mathematically equivalent to A = P(1 + r/n)nt for the principal, plus the standard future-value-of-an-annuity math for contributions — but transparent enough that you can check any single year's row by hand.

Important: This Is a Math Tool, Not Financial Advice

This calculator assumes your rate of return stays perfectly constant for the entire period you enter — real investments never behave that way. Stock markets, funds, and savings rates fluctuate year to year, sometimes losing money entirely. Use this tool to understand how compounding math works, not as a prediction or promise of what any specific investment will actually return. For real financial decisions, consider speaking with a licensed financial advisor.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the interest already earned in previous periods. Instead of earning a flat amount each period like simple interest, your balance grows faster over time because each period's interest is calculated on a larger base.

How is compound interest different from simple interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal for the whole term (Interest = P × r × t), so it grows in a straight line. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus all previously earned interest, so it grows faster the longer you leave money invested — the gap between the two widens every year.

Does compounding frequency really matter?

Yes, but modestly. At the same nominal annual rate, compounding more often (daily vs. annually, for example) slightly raises the effective annual return because interest starts earning its own interest sooner. The difference is usually a fraction of a percentage point per year, not a dramatic change.

How much do regular contributions help?

A lot, over long periods. Contributing a fixed amount every month or year means each new contribution has time to compound on its own, so a modest habit sustained for years can end up contributing more to your final balance than the initial lump sum did.

What formula does this calculator use?

It simulates growth period by period: each compounding period, interest is added to the current balance at rate ÷ compounding frequency, then any contributions due in that period are added. This matches the standard A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) formula for the principal, plus the correct future-value-of-annuity math for contributions, without you needing to trust a single black-box formula.

Is this a guarantee of investment returns?

No. This tool performs a pure mathematical projection assuming a constant, unchanging rate of return — it is not investment advice and does not predict or guarantee what any real investment will do. Actual markets fluctuate, rates change, and past performance never guarantees future results.
