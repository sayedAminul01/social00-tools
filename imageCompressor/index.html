How Lossy Image Compression Works

JPEG and WebP are lossy formats: instead of storing every pixel exactly, they discard detail the human eye barely notices — subtle color gradients, high-frequency texture, noise — to shrink the file. The quality setting controls how aggressively that detail gets thrown away. Higher quality keeps more of the original data and produces a bigger file; lower quality discards more and produces a smaller one. It's a direct trade-off, not magic compression that costs nothing.

JPEG vs WebP: Which Should You Choose?

WebP uses a newer compression algorithm and generally produces noticeably smaller files than JPEG at the same visual quality — often 25-35% smaller. JPEG has near-universal support: every browser, image editor, social platform and printer handles it without a second thought. WebP support in modern browsers is excellent, but if you need maximum compatibility with older tools or systems, JPEG is still the safer default.

Why Smaller Images Matter for Speed & SEO

Images are typically the heaviest assets on a web page. Bloated images directly slow down Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) — one of Google's named Core Web Vitals and a real, documented ranking factor. Compressing your images before you upload them shrinks page weight, speeds up load times, improves LCP scores, and reduces bandwidth costs for you and your visitors.

Recommended Quality Settings

For most web photography, 60-80% quality is the sweet spot — a large reduction in file size with virtually no visible loss. Drop to 30-50% for thumbnails, background images or anywhere fine detail doesn't matter. Reserve 85-95% for hero banners, print-adjacent work, or portfolio images where every detail counts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does compressing an image reduce its quality?

Yes, if you use JPEG or WebP output. Both are lossy formats, so compression trades some fine detail for a smaller file size. At 60-80% quality the difference is usually invisible, but it's a real tradeoff, not a free lunch.

What's the difference between JPEG and WebP?

WebP generally produces smaller files than JPEG at equivalent visual quality thanks to a newer compression algorithm. JPEG has near-universal support everywhere; WebP support is excellent in modern browsers but occasionally missing in older or niche software.

Is my image uploaded anywhere?

No. This tool is 100% client-side — your image is loaded, resized and re-encoded entirely on your own device using the browser's Canvas API, and is never sent to any server.

What's the best quality setting for web images?

60-80% is the sweet spot for most photos. Go lower (30-50%) for thumbnails or background images, and higher (85-95%) for hero images or detail-critical photography.

Can I compress PNG files?

Yes — you can upload a PNG, but the compressed output is re-encoded as JPEG or WebP since those formats support an adjustable lossy quality slider. This is expected and usually shrinks PNGs significantly.

Is this tool free?

Yes — completely free, no account required, no watermark, and no limit on how many images you compress. Everything runs locally in your browser.
